# Day 104: Stop Copying the Market Leader's GEO Strategy

The market leader and the challenger do not have the same GEO job.

That sounds obvious until the dashboard appears. A CMO sees an incumbent named across broad category questions, comparison questions, procurement questions, and generic "best provider" answers. The response is tempting: copy the incumbent's content footprint, reproduce its prompt list, target the same broad coverage, and hope the answer engines eventually treat the challenger as a smaller version of the leader.

That is usually an expensive way to remain interchangeable.

A leader may need to defend accurate, broad category understanding because buyers already expect it to appear. A challenger has a different problem. It needs to find the buyer situation where its real advantage is relevant enough to change the answer, not chase every question the incumbent can credibly occupy.

For CMOs, Marketing Directors, and founders, the practical question is not "How do we match the leader everywhere?" It is "Which broad questions are we maintaining because we already own the category, and which narrow buyer situation is commercially worth winning because the incumbent cannot credibly own it?"

## The same result can mean different work

Imagine a generalised B2B category with one established incumbent and one credible challenger.

Across broad category questions, answer-led surfaces often describe the incumbent first. It has more public explanation, more historical language around the category, more comparison pages, more third-party mentions, and more familiar buyer shorthand. In a bounded research run, it may appear as the default category anchor: the provider used to explain what the category is, how buyers compare options, and which risks matter.

The challenger may appear less often. When it does appear, it may be attached to a narrower situation: a specific use case, a geography, a technical constraint, a service model, an integration path, a speed requirement, or a buyer type that finds the leader too heavy, too generic, too slow, or too expensive.

A lazy read says: "The challenger is behind. Publish more. Track more prompts. Close the gap."

A better read asks what role each brand is playing.

If the leader is the broad incumbent, its GEO work is defensive maintenance. It needs the category story to stay accurate, current, and properly bounded across the questions it is expected to answer.

If the challenger is a specialist exception, its GEO work is selective expansion. It should turn that exception into a commercially useful territory, then make the public explanation strong enough for answer engines to repeat without flattening it into generic category language.

Those are not the same brief.

## Leader and challenger objectives side by side

| Strategic position | What answer-led research may show | Sensible GEO objective | Budget mistake to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Category leader | Named as the default category anchor across broad buyer questions | Maintain broad, accurate, up-to-date category legibility | Treating every challenger mention as a crisis |
| Broad incumbent | Recommended often, but with visible caveats around price, complexity, region, fit, or speed | Clarify fit boundaries so broad visibility does not create poor-fit demand | Pretending broad presence means every buyer context is winnable |
| Challenger | Mentioned occasionally, often in a narrower context | Own a specific buyer situation where the advantage is credible | Copying the leader's entire question map |
| Specialist exception | Recommended when the answer adds a constraint such as integration, sector, urgency, compliance, geography, or team size | Make that constraint publicly legible and easy to cite | Expanding too fast into generic category claims |
| Adjacent substitute | Appears as a different way to solve the problem | Decide whether to compete with the category or reframe around the substitute outcome | Measuring only named competitors and missing the alternative path |
| Omitted option | Does not appear in relevant answers | Diagnose whether the gap is category language, public explanation, source availability, or genuine poor fit | Assuming absence proves lack of demand or permanent exclusion |

This table is not a universal taxonomy of a market. It is a way to keep a research run honest.

One ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity, Gemini, or Google AI feature answer is not market truth. A small answer-led sample does not reveal demand, causality, sales performance, or platform-wide consensus. It can, however, show the role a brand appears to occupy in a particular question set on a particular date.

The same observation can therefore create opposite actions: a leader may maintain or constrain, while a challenger may concentrate or test.

That role should shape the next experiment.

## Do not copy the incumbent's question territory

The leader's question territory is often broad because the buyer already uses it as a reference point. "Best tools for X", "how to choose X", "X versus Y", "enterprise X providers", "implementation risks for X", and "alternatives to [leader]" may all be reasonable places for the leader to defend clarity.

For a challenger, the same territory can be a trap.

Broad category questions usually reward the public material that already explains the category in familiar terms. If the challenger simply publishes a thinner version of the leader's pages, it gives answer engines another near-identical source. The result is rarely a stronger strategic position. More often, the challenger becomes a minor option in a list shaped by the incumbent's language.

The better challenger question is narrower:

- Which buyer situation makes the leader's default answer feel overbuilt, slow, risky, expensive, unsuitable, or incomplete?
- Which constraint does the challenger actually solve better?
- Which public pages, comparisons, examples, documentation, and third-party references make that constraint clear?
- Which answer surfaces already hint at this role, even if inconsistently?
- Which broad questions should the challenger ignore for now because winning them would require budget without commercial focus?

The difference from ordinary niche selection is the evidence loop: the territory is not chosen because the business wants a smaller market; it is chosen because a recorded buyer question shows the incumbent's broad answer is weak, the challenger has a real constraint-level advantage, and public material can make that advantage repeatable.

The challenger is not trying to become a smaller incumbent. It is trying to become the obvious answer when the incumbent's broad fit is not enough.

## Leaders should not react like challengers

The leader has a different failure mode.

If a category anchor overreacts to every specialist mention, it can turn broad category clarity into defensive sprawl. Teams start producing pages for every edge case, every feature phrase, every comparison, and every small competitor. The public corpus becomes noisy. The buyer has more words to process, but not necessarily a better understanding of when the leader is the right fit.

A leader's first obligation is to keep the main category understanding accurate:

- Do answer-led surfaces describe the category correctly?
- Are the leader's core use cases, constraints, and fit boundaries current?
- Are old claims, retired features, unsupported geographies, or stale partner references still being repeated?
- Do comparison answers explain real trade-offs, or do they collapse the category into a vague list?
- Are Google-visible pages, documentation, comparison material, and entity descriptions aligned with what the business can actually deliver?

For Google specifically, keep the caveat intact: Google's AI features rely on core Search ranking and quality systems. `llms.txt`, special AI markup, arbitrary chunking, and over-focused structured data are not required switches for Google AI visibility.

The leader's work is not to block every challenger from ever appearing. That is neither realistic nor healthy. The work is to make sure broad category visibility does not drift into inaccurate understanding.

## Turn the competitive read into an allocation choice

A useful competitive read separates the leader brief from the challenger brief before the research starts.

For each surface and question group, record four things.

First, the question territory. Is the answer about broad category education, comparison, implementation risk, pricing or procurement, switching, integration, regional fit, regulated use, specialist need, or post-purchase support?

Second, the attributed fit. Why is each brand named? Is it described as comprehensive, familiar, enterprise-ready, fast, specialised, affordable, flexible, technically deep, regionally relevant, or easier to adopt?

Third, the constraints. What conditions make the recommendation stronger or weaker? Team size, budget, existing stack, compliance requirement, service expectation, time to value, geography, and operational maturity can all change the answer.

Fourth, the recommendation context. Is the brand the default anchor, a broad incumbent, a specialist exception, a niche challenger, an adjacent substitute, or simply absent from a question where it should credibly appear?

Then choose the work according to position.

A leader might maintain a small set of high-volume category explanations, comparison pages, documentation updates, and entity descriptions so broad answers remain correct.

A challenger might select five to ten commercially important questions where the leader is present but not ideally fitted, then build the clearest public explanation of the specific constraint it solves. That could mean a focused comparison page, a use-case page with concrete fit boundaries, a technical explanation, public documentation, an integration note, or a buyer-facing article that names the trade-off without pretending to be the whole market.

The output is not a single visibility score. It is an allocation choice.

## The CMO budget consequence

The budget consequence is simple: leaders fund maintenance of category understanding; challengers fund selective ownership of buyer context.

If a challenger spends the quarter imitating the leader's full coverage map, it may buy a larger content backlog without earning a stronger role in the answer. The team can feel busy while the market still reads it as another option.

If the same challenger uses bounded answer-led research to find where the leader is overbroad and the challenger is genuinely better fitted, the spend becomes sharper. The team can ignore low-value broad coverage, improve the pages that explain its advantage, and track whether the narrow territory becomes more consistently represented over time.

If a leader spends the quarter chasing every specialist mention, it may dilute the category story. If it protects the main buyer questions and keeps fit boundaries honest, it can stay broadly legible without pretending every edge case belongs to it.

That is the strategic asymmetry.

GEO is not a race to copy the biggest public footprint in the category. It is the discipline of making the right role legible to answer-led surfaces and to the human buyer who uses them.

## The leadership question

Before approving the next GEO sprint, ask one question:

Are we defending the role we already hold, or trying to win a narrower role the market leader cannot credibly own?

If the answer is unclear, pause the content backlog. Run the position-aware read first. Then fund the work your position can actually win, instead of borrowing the incumbent's coverage map and calling it ambition.
