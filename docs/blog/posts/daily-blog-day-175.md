# Day 175: Budget for the Buying Season, Not a Flat Year

A buying guide delivered after the shortlist closes can be useful and still miss the sale it was funded to support.

An annual content allowance does not prevent that mistake. The team can deliver every promised article while putting the preparation after the decision.

For a CMO funding GEO, I would start with the customer's buying window, then work backwards to the research and material that need to be ready. That means buying different work at different points in the year—not simply ordering the same monthly output more urgently when demand arrives.

The calendar below is a proposed planning approach for Zero-Shot Agency, not a tested programme or an observed seasonal campaign.

<!-- more -->

## Put one buying window on the calendar

Imagine a fictional supplier selling a winter lighting-hire package to independent venues. For this example only, stipulate that its target buyers research options in July and August, select suppliers in September and October, and use the hired equipment in November and December. January to June is comparatively quiet for new purchases.

Those dates are invented conditions, not evidence about the lighting industry. The offer stays the same throughout the illustration; the customer's immediate decision changes.

Here is the investment calendar I would propose:

| Period in this fictional year | Buyer's immediate question | Work I would fund | Work I would defer |
|---|---|---|---|
| January–June: quiet purchasing period | “Would hiring suit our venue next winter?” | Maintain essential offer information; investigate next season's requirements towards the end of this period | A standing quota of new seasonal articles |
| July–August: options research | “What can we hire, and what preparation would our venue need?” | Have the core hire-versus-buy and venue-fit material ready for this phase; reserve effort for useful clarification | Minor variations of the same broad introduction |
| September–October: supplier selection | “Can this package meet our date, installation needs and budget?” | Keep current terms clear and address purchasing questions within the existing material | A large new awareness series aimed at buyers still discovering the category |
| November–December: equipment in use | “How do we use and return what we hired?” | Support the current customer job; retain lessons for the next cycle | Treating support demand as an invitation to expand acquisition publishing |

This is not a recommendation to switch the website off between seasons. It changes the emphasis of discretionary work while preserving useful information and customer support.

## Spend before the choice, not only during the peak

In this scenario, commissioning the foundational comparison in September would put its production inside the supplier-selection window. I would instead fund that preparation before July, when the stipulated research phase begins.

That creates a real trade-off. Earlier preparation commits money before purchasing activity confirms the season's strength. Waiting preserves cash and may reveal newer requirements, but leaves less time to help people who are already comparing options.

The sensible lead time depends on the work: researching requirements, obtaining product answers, writing, review and publication. This calendar supplies no universally optimal number of weeks. Nor does early publication guarantee that an answer engine will retrieve or cite the material.

Reserve some effort for the active buying period rather than spending everything on advance production. A comparison prepared early cannot anticipate every purchasing question, and the supplier's terms may change. The plan should allow useful updates without making the busiest period responsible for creating the entire explanation from scratch.

## Give GEO questions a season too

For this offer, “Should we hire or buy?” and “Which hire package fits this installation date?” belong to different moments. The research brief should cover both without assuming they deserve identical attention all year.

Google describes AI Mode as supporting exploration and complex comparisons. Its AI features may issue related searches across subtopics and sources when developing responses.[1] That supports preparing useful material around connected buyer questions; it does not tell us how often this fictional audience asks them, or when.

If Google Trends is used to investigate timing, keep its role narrow. Google describes sampled search requests, normalised by time and geography and scaled from zero to one hundred.[2] That is relative Google search interest—not absolute buyer volume, ChatGPT demand or sales. No Trends chart was collected for this calendar.

Actual purchasing dates need a separate business basis. Where appropriately available, past enquiry and order timing can inform the plan; a search-interest peak cannot substitute for that history. Without an established buying window, treat the schedule as a hypothesis rather than move the whole budget around it.

## Let the quiet period cost less

The uncomfortable part of seasonal planning is accepting an uneven production schedule.

For this fictional offer, I would reduce new acquisition material during the quiet period, prepare before options research, and retain room for purchasing support while buyers choose. If the offer or buying window changes, revise that allocation rather than defend last year's calendar.

The commercial test is not whether every month received equal attention. It is whether the work was ready while the customer could still use it to decide.
