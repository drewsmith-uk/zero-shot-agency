# Day 149: The Buyer Has a Photo, Not a Keyword

A buyer can recognise the thing they need without knowing what the industry calls it.

They may have a photograph of a connector left behind from an exhibition stand, a component seen in another workspace, or a detail cropped from a supplier's installation. The object is concrete. The category vocabulary is missing.

That creates a discovery journey that does not begin with a keyword. It begins with an image.

For companies selling tangible products, components, and design-led goods, this matters commercially. A category page built only around expert terminology assumes the buyer already knows enough to ask the expert question. Visual search can give that buyer an earlier entrance: from object, to possible name, to a more informed investigation.

## The search begins before category language

Google says Lens can be used to learn more about an image or objects around the user. Its image-search results can include AI Overviews, search results for objects in the image, similar images, and websites containing the same or a similar image.[1] In Chrome, Google gives examples including selecting an image of a shirt to find where to buy it, and lets users select content on a page for a Lens search.[2]

Those are documented Google capabilities, not a promise about what any particular photograph will return. They also do not establish equivalent behaviour in ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity, or other products.

The useful shift is at the input. In ordinary keyword planning, the team starts with words a buyer might type. In object-first discovery, the first question is different:

> What might the buyer be able to show before they know what to call it?

The buyer has not supplied a text query for a system to expand. They are using the visible object to acquire the vocabulary needed for the next stage of research.

## A hypothetical object-first journey

Consider an explicitly hypothetical, non-safety-critical example. A Marketing Manager inherits parts from a reusable exhibition display but has no component list. One small connector is missing. She has a clear photograph of an intact connector, but not the manufacturer, system family, or accepted product name.

She searches with the image. A visual-search result might suggest that the object belongs to a class of modular exhibition display connectors. That phrase is hypothetical teaching vocabulary, not a captured Google result. It gives her a possible category to investigate, not a purchase answer.

She can now ask better questions: Which connector types serve this function? Which details distinguish one system family from another? What measurements, materials, markings, or documentation would a supplier need before identifying a replacement?

The commercial opportunity appears before the final supplier comparison. A manufacturer or distributor can help the buyer cross from “I have this object” to “I understand what kind of thing I am investigating.” That may bring category-naive demand into view earlier than a page written only for buyers who already know the catalogue language.

## Treat the image as an entrance, not proof

Visual resemblance cannot establish compatibility. Two components can look alike while differing in dimensions, load limits, locking method, material, system generation, or manufacturer specification. An image-led result must not be presented as evidence that a part is safe, available, interchangeable, or suitable.

The image should open the investigation, not conclude it.

For publishers, Google's image guidance says the content and metadata of the page embedding an image can influence how and where that image may appear in Search. It recommends placing images near relevant text on pages relevant to the subject, and choosing a preferred image that is relevant and representative of the page.[3]

That does not turn image publishing into a visibility switch. It does suggest a sound product-content principle: show the real object clearly and explain it in the context where a buyer can learn its accepted name, function, boundaries, and next verification step.

## Give one object a discovery brief

This is not equally valuable for every business. A consultancy whose offer has no distinctive physical object should not manufacture a visual-search programme to follow the trend.

For a company with tangible products, choose one commercially meaningful object that buyers may encounter before they know its category. Write a short discovery brief:

1. What can the buyer photograph or crop?
2. Which category language might they be trying to learn?
3. Which visible details help narrow the investigation?
4. Which facts must be verified before fit or compatibility can be claimed?
5. Which page can explain the object without pretending to identify every photographed example?

No live experiment or customer result supports this post. The proposal is narrower: add object-first discovery to the planning conversation where the product makes it relevant.

Sometimes demand arrives as a well-formed question. Sometimes it arrives as a photograph of a thing the buyer cannot yet name.

If your discovery strategy only begins once the keyword exists, you may be entering the journey one step too late.
