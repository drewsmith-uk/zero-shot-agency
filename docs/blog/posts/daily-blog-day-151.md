# Day 151: Define the Paid Intervention After the Answer

A prospective buyer asks for guidance on a difficult business problem. An AI response explains the common options, gives them a checklist and links to useful sources. They arrive at a consultancy site already understanding the basics.

That does not prove the consultancy has been replaced. It does expose a weak offer if the paid proposition is only “we know the explanation”.

Google describes AI Mode as capable of answering questions that might previously have required multiple searches, including comparisons, with follow-up questions and links for further exploration.[1] That is a documented Google capability, not evidence that every buyer receives a sufficient answer, trusts it or stops buying advice.

The commercial design question is narrower: **after the general explanation has been supplied, what is the buyer paying the provider to judge, change or deliver?**

<!-- more -->

## Educational content and paid work need different jobs

Useful public content can explain terminology, frame choices and help a buyer ask a better question. In answer-led discovery, parts of that explanation may be summarised before the buyer visits the source.

Trying to preserve demand by withholding the basics is the wrong response. It makes the company harder to understand while leaving the paid unit undefined.

The service should begin where general explanation runs out: inside the buyer's constraints, evidence, trade-offs and authority. That may involve diagnosing a specific situation, selecting between defensible options, challenging an internal assumption, facilitating a consequential decision or carrying out an authorised change.

Not every engagement needs implementation. Advisory-only work remains valuable when contextual judgement is itself the bounded deliverable. The distinction is not “advice versus execution”. It is general information versus a defined intervention for this buyer.

## A fictional offer before the teardown

Consider an explicitly fictional customer-research consultancy. This is not a ZSA package, client engagement or observed market result.

Its public offer says:

> We help B2B companies run better customer interviews through expert guidance, proven frameworks and tailored workshops.

The language sounds professional, but the chargeable unit is difficult to see. A buyer can already find interview structures, question-writing guidance and workshop templates. “Tailored” signals context without saying what the consultancy will examine, decide or produce.

Now give the fictional buyer a real decision: a product team must choose whether to redesign onboarding before its next release. It has support complaints, six recent interview transcripts and disagreement about the cause of early abandonment. Another generic interview guide will not settle that disagreement.

The consultancy can now define a paid intervention:

- examine the supplied complaints, transcripts and current onboarding assumptions;
- identify where the evidence supports, weakens or fails to resolve the competing explanations;
- facilitate one decision session with the authorised product and customer leads;
- deliver a short decision brief naming the recommended next research or product action, the evidence behind it and the material uncertainty that remains.

The provider is not promising a successful release, a conversion increase or universal truth. The buyer retains the product decision and any implementation authority. What the buyer purchases is a bounded piece of contextual judgement that changes the quality of one named decision.

## Package the intervention, not artificial scarcity

A stronger service page would not need to hide its interview guidance. It could publish the general method clearly, allowing buyers and answer-led systems to understand the consultancy's subject and approach.

The paid offer would then specify five things the public explanation cannot complete on its own:

1. **Buyer-specific inputs:** which evidence and constraints enter the work.
2. **Contextual judgement:** what the provider will assess rather than merely explain.
3. **Authorised intervention:** what the provider may challenge, facilitate, recommend or change.
4. **Bounded deliverable:** what the buyer receives, and where the work stops.
5. **Decision use:** which real choice the output is intended to improve.

Those elements do not guarantee demand or outcomes. They make the purchasing unit inspectable. A buyer can decide whether the intervention matches the decision, whether the required access is acceptable and whether the boundary is worth paying for.

This also gives GEO and offer design separate responsibilities. Public education can make the provider relevant to the question. The commercial page must make the paid work distinct from the answer to that question. Visibility cannot repair an offer whose only scarce asset was access to a general explanation.

## Rewrite the paid unit in one sentence

The fictional offer began as:

> Expert guidance, proven frameworks and tailored workshops for better customer interviews.

After the teardown, the paid unit becomes:

> For one product decision, we assess your existing customer evidence, test the competing explanations with the authorised team, and deliver a decision brief stating the supported next action and the uncertainty that remains; product approval and implementation stay with you.

The first version sells access to expertise. The second names the context, intervention, output, decision and boundary.

When an AI response can supply the opening explanation, do not make the explanation less useful. Make the paid intervention more explicit.
