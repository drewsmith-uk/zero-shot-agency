# Day 157: An AI Adoption Headline Is Not a B2B Channel Plan

Consider this invented survey statement: *“Most enterprise knowledge workers use generative AI for work tasks.”* It is not an actual survey finding, and no budget decision reported here took place. It is a test sentence for a common leap in channel planning: if AI use is widespread at work, should a B2B company move acquisition budget into generative engine optimization?

Not from this statement alone. It describes neither the specific buyers a company needs to reach nor the activities those buyers perform while choosing suppliers.

<!-- more -->

## Read what the statement measures

Start with the population: “enterprise knowledge workers.” That population may include people with purchasing authority, people who influence purchases, and people with no role in buying the relevant category. An aggregate result for the whole group does not isolate any of them.

This is a fit question, not a sample-size question. A large sample can estimate the behaviour of its defined population precisely while still being the wrong population for a particular commercial decision. The [American Association for Public Opinion Research’s survey guidance](https://aapor.org/standards-and-ethics/best-practices/) recommends designing a sample to match the population being studied. Applying that principle here means asking whether the reported population corresponds to the target buyers behind the proposed channel change. The invented statement does not establish that correspondence.

Now read the activity: “use generative AI for work tasks.” AAPOR also recommends that questions be specific and ask about one concept at a time. “Work tasks” can cover many activities. It does not identify category discovery, supplier comparison, shortlist creation or purchase research.

Those activities are not interchangeable measurements. Someone can use an AI assistant for drafting without using it to find a supplier. Someone else can use it for both. The statement gives us no basis for choosing between those possibilities.

Finally, read what is missing. The statement does not provide the exact question wording, target population, recruitment method, field dates or limitations. AAPOR’s [Transparency Initiative disclosure elements](https://aapor.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/TI-Attachment-C.pdf) identify those details as information readers need to evaluate survey results. Disclosure would help us interpret the finding; it would not turn general workplace use into evidence of B2B supplier discovery.

## Draw the budget boundary

The relevant decision is concrete: should a marketing leader reduce an established acquisition channel to fund more GEO activity because of this adoption statement?

The answer is no—not because the statement proves that buyers avoid AI, but because it does not establish that the relevant buying population uses AI for the relevant discovery activity. The same evidence also cannot justify the opposite claim that existing buyer behaviour is unchanged.

A bounded GEO exploration may still be justified by other evidence or strategic priorities. That case should stand on its own. The adoption headline can describe a broad technology pattern; it cannot carry a category-specific channel reallocation.

For CMOs, Marketing Directors and founders, the distinction protects both sides of the decision. It avoids treating every AI user as a buyer in market, while leaving room to investigate emerging discovery routes without pretending that a general usage statement has already measured them.

The practical rule is simple: match the population and the activity in the evidence to the population and the activity in the budget decision. If the statement measures workforce use while the decision concerns B2B supplier discovery, the missing link is not a footnote. It is the question the budget depends on.
