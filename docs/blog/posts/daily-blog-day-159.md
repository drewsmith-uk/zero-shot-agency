# Day 159: Build the Demo Around the Moment the Buyer Needs

A buyer arrives with one task: see how the product handles an exception.

The usual product demonstration makes her earn that answer. First comes the company story, then the dashboard tour, then the standard workflow. The relevant sequence appears six minutes later—if she is still watching.

A more useful commission treats the demonstration as a set of task-specific moments. The recording still has a coherent narrative, but each meaningful segment also has a label and a destination. A researcher can start where her question starts.

This is an annotated storyboard for a fictional B2B product, not a published ZSA experiment or a report of observed search behaviour. The timestamps are invented positions.

## Storyboard the buyer's inspection tasks

Imagine a procurement workflow product being evaluated by an Operations Director. The full demonstration is eight minutes long.

**00:00 — Orient the workflow**

Show the request, the people involved and the outcome they are trying to reach: “A department needs to buy from a new supplier without losing the approval record.” This establishes the full narrative, but a direct arrival later in the video will have skipped it. Every independently addressable segment therefore needs a short spoken reset or visible caption that restores the minimum case and state.

**01:05 — Review a supplier exception**

Open with a caption that identifies the new-supplier request and the unresolved exception. Then show which information the reviewer receives, what remains unresolved and where supporting evidence appears. The label describes the buyer's task, rather than the interface name “Exception module”.

**03:10 — Route the approval**

Start by restating that the supplier exception is still pending and now needs finance and security review. Demonstrate what happens when those teams need different evidence, including what the next person can see. “Route an approval across two teams” is more useful than “Advanced workflow”.

**05:20 — Retrieve the decision record**

State that the supplier has now been approved before moving forward in time. Then show how someone answers: who approved this supplier, against which evidence, and when? This is a distinct inspection task. A buyer interested in auditability should not have to infer it from the approval screen.

**07:05 — Define the next evaluation step**

Remind the viewer that this fictional case covered one supplier exception, then mark what was shown, what remains configuration-dependent and what a serious evaluator should test with their own process. The destination is a useful next step, not a generic sales outro.

The labels force an editorial decision before recording. Each segment must answer a recognisable buyer question. If the team cannot name the task, it probably has a screen sequence rather than a useful chapter.

## Publish the route the asset can actually support

Google documents “key moments” as a video Search feature that lets people navigate segments like chapters. Google may detect segments automatically, while publishers can provide important points through supported structured data or, for a YouTube-hosted video, timestamps and labels in the YouTube description.[1]

Those routes are related, but they are not interchangeable instructions. A YouTube demonstration can use its own timestamped description and watch destination. A video hosted on a company's site may, where appropriate, have a dedicated watch page and use the supported video markup route for that implementation. Google's guidance says a watch page should make one video the main reason for visiting, rather than burying it inside a page where the video is incidental.[2]

The commissioning principle comes first: record clean segment boundaries, give each segment an accurate task label, and send the viewer to a destination that can open the relevant moment. Metadata cannot rescue a demonstration whose useful answer is scattered across the introduction, three screens and the outro.

## Where the video enters Search

When those moments are published through a supported YouTube description or eligible video structured data, they give Google information it may use for ordinary video Search features. That does not establish that chapters improve conversion, cause an AI citation or make a video visible across ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity or Gemini.

Google's guidance for AI Overviews and AI Mode says normal SEO foundations remain relevant and that there are no additional requirements or special optimisations for appearing in those features.[3] Video chapters are therefore not an AI-ranking switch. They are a search-adjacent way to make time-based material more addressable within a documented Google feature.

That boundary still leaves a valuable job for marketing leaders. Commission the product demonstration around the capabilities buyers need to inspect. Preserve enough context for a direct arrival. Label the moments in the buyer's language. Then publish each route through the destination the actual video platform supports.

A product demonstration should be more than a linear pitch with a play button. It can become a small map of buyer tasks—and let the researcher begin at the moment that matters.
